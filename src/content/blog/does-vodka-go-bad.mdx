# Does Vodka Go Bad? How Long Vodka Really Lasts

No, vodka does not go bad in the way perishable foods or drinks like wine and beer do. An unopened bottle of vodka lasts essentially indefinitely, and an opened bottle keeps its quality for many years — often decades — with only a very slow fade in flavor and aroma over time. Thanks to its roughly 40% alcohol content, vodka is inhospitable to the bacteria and spoilage organisms that ruin other beverages.

That means the bottle of [Touch Vodka](/products) in your cabinet is almost certainly fine to drink, whether you opened it last month or several years ago. Below, we explain exactly why vodka is so stable, how long it really lasts opened versus unopened, and the few signs that something has actually gone wrong.

## Does Vodka Expire? The Short Answer

Vodka doesn't have a true expiration date. Unlike milk, juice, or even wine and beer, distilled spirits are shelf-stable because their high alcohol concentration acts as a natural preservative. You may notice a date printed on some bottles, but for spirits this is typically a "best by" or production reference rather than a hard expiry.

### Why Vodka Doesn't Spoil

The reason comes down to chemistry. Vodka is bottled at around **80 proof (40% ABV)**, and at that concentration:

- **Bacteria and microbes can't survive.** Alcohol above roughly 20% ABV inhibits the growth of the organisms that cause food and drink to spoil.
- **There are no sugars or proteins to ferment or rot.** Vodka is a clean, neutral spirit, so there's nothing for spoilage organisms to feed on.
- **It's chemically stable.** Properly sealed, vodka isn't exposed to the oxidation that gradually degrades wine.

This is fundamentally different from wine and beer, which have lower alcohol levels, residual sugars, and live or reactive components that make them perishable.

## How Long Does Unopened Vodka Last?

An unopened, properly sealed bottle of vodka lasts **indefinitely**. Stored away from heat and direct sunlight, a sealed bottle can sit untouched for decades and remain perfectly good to drink. The flavor you experience on opening it will be virtually identical to the day it was bottled.

This is why vodka makes such a reliable spirit to keep on hand or give as a gift — there's no rush to use it before it "turns." A sealed bottle of [Touch Artisan](/products/touch-artisan) bought today will be just as enjoyable years from now.

## How Long Does Opened Vodka Last?

Once you break the seal, vodka still lasts an exceptionally long time — generally **many years**, and realistically decades, before any change becomes noticeable. Opening the bottle introduces air, which over a very long period can cause a slow, gradual fade in aroma and a slight softening of flavor through evaporation and minor oxidation. But this happens so slowly that most bottles are finished long before it matters.

### What Actually Changes Over Time

| Time Since Opening | What to Expect |
|---|---|
| First few years | No perceptible change in taste or aroma |
| Many years | Very subtle softening of aroma; flavor still good |
| Decades | Possible faint loss of intensity if poorly sealed |

The biggest factor isn't time itself but how well the bottle is sealed and stored. A tightly recapped bottle kept upright, away from heat and light, will hold its quality far longer than one left half-empty with a loose cap in a warm, sunny spot.

### Flavored Vodka Is Slightly Different

Flavored expressions like [Touch Key Lime](/products/touch-key-lime) or [Touch Orange](/products/touch-orange) contain natural flavoring, so their aromatic top notes can fade a little faster than unflavored vodka once opened. They're still safe to drink for years, but for the brightest flavor, enjoy opened flavored bottles within a couple of years.

## How to Tell If Vodka Has Gone Bad

Because vodka is so stable, genuine spoilage is rare — but a few signs indicate a bottle has been compromised, usually by contamination or poor storage rather than age:

- **Cloudiness or floating particles** in a normally crystal-clear spirit.
- **An off, sour, or chemical smell** that's distinctly different from the clean alcohol aroma.
- **A strange or unpleasant taste** if you take a small, cautious sip.
- **A damaged or compromised seal** that may have let in contaminants.

If a bottle shows these signs, it's best to discard it. In the vast majority of cases, though, your vodka is perfectly fine. The clean, neutral character of a well-made spirit — the kind you get from [our distillation process](/our-story) — is itself a sign of stability.

## How to Make Your Vodka Last Even Longer

You don't need to do much, but a few simple habits keep an opened bottle at its best:

- **Reseal it tightly** after every pour to limit air exposure.
- **Store it upright** so the spirit isn't in constant contact with the cap.
- **Keep it cool, dark, and away from heat** — a cabinet or pantry is ideal.
- **Avoid temperature swings**, which can stress the seal over time.

Freezer storage is also perfectly fine — vodka won't freeze at 40% ABV — and it's a popular stylistic choice. For a full breakdown of fridge, freezer, and shelf storage, see our guide on [how to store vodka](/blog/how-to-store-vodka).

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can old vodka make you sick?

No. Vodka doesn't grow harmful bacteria thanks to its high alcohol content, so age alone won't make a properly sealed and stored bottle unsafe. The only real risk comes from obvious contamination — a broken seal, cloudiness, or an off smell — in which case you should discard it. An old but clean, clear, normal-smelling bottle is safe to drink.

### Does vodka go bad after opening?

Not in any practical sense. Opened vodka keeps its quality for many years, with only a very slow fade in aroma and flavor over decades. Reseal it tightly and store it cool and dark, and you can enjoy it long after opening.

### Why doesn't vodka expire like wine or beer?

Wine and beer have lower alcohol levels plus sugars and other reactive components that allow them to oxidize, ferment, or spoil. Vodka's roughly 40% ABV prevents microbial growth, and as a neutral spirit it has nothing for spoilage organisms to feed on, so it stays stable far longer.

### Should I refrigerate or freeze vodka to preserve it?

You don't need to — room-temperature storage in a cool, dark place preserves vodka indefinitely. That said, refrigerating or freezing vodka is perfectly safe (it won't freeze at 40% ABV) and many people prefer the thicker, chilled texture for sipping. It's a matter of preference, not preservation.
